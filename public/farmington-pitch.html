⚠️ PFAS — Know Your Sources

Standard targeted PFAS panels cover 7–40 compounds. The fluorinated universe in your system is far larger.

350+
Fluorinated Compounds Detected
in a Single AIMS Sample Run

AIMS detects all fluorinated signatures simultaneously — perfluoroalkyls, polyfluoroalkyls, precursors, and unknowns — in the same 25mL sample used for everything else. The critical question isn't just whether PFAS is present. It's where it's coming from and whether your treatment process is actually reducing it.

The Monitoring Imperative. The regulatory landscape around PFAS is evolving — EPA recently proposed rescinding some compound-specific MCLs while maintaining others. Regardless of enforcement thresholds, knowing your PFAS sources, concentrations, and treatment efficiency is essential for defensible operations, downstream protection, and permit compliance.

The Case for Moving First

Four Converging Forces

1. New Leadership, New Mandate

City Manager Shaña Reeves arrived with a 22-year institutional track record and a drive to modernize Farmington's operations — and inherited two regulatory violation notices. AIMS gives her the forward-looking data infrastructure to demonstrate proactive, next-generation governance from the start.

2. Monitoring Obligations Are Expanding

Draft permit NM0031135 brings new requirements. NMED has already cited Farmington twice this year. And as the PFAS regulatory landscape continues to evolve — with some standards being revisited — the operators who have documented their own baseline data and source attribution are the ones with defensible positions.

3. The Drug Crisis Demands New Tools

108,400 fentanyl pills. Seven overdoses in county detention. Twenty trafficking operations. San Juan County is at the epicenter of New Mexico's fentanyl epidemic. Wastewater epidemiology is now endorsed by CDC, SAMHSA, and the White House ONDCP. Farmington can lead.

4. Environmental Justice Obligation

The San Juan River is sacred and essential to Navajo Nation communities downstream. Documenting what Farmington's WWTP is — and isn't — removing protects the city from future liability and is the right thing to do.
